# Sori V2: Decision Council

**Decision:** Which additions should move Sori from a polished first slice toward a useful, launchable beginner app while preserving free access?

**Context carried from the brief:** Sori is a personal project for the creator and potentially young learners and Korean Americans. The required first course includes Hangul, grammar, vocabulary, and speaking. The non-negotiable is that learning stays free. The creator wants a dynamic frontend, a TypeScript backend, a strong database, and exceptional visuals. The 12-month ambition is a launchable app with many users; no numeric target, hosting provider, budget, or launch date has been set. The current app uses Next.js, NestJS, and PostgreSQL, with browser-local progress when its optional API is not configured.

These are simulated adviser lenses generated by one assistant. They are useful ways to stress-test the choice, not independent people, research, or corroboration.

## First-pass perspectives

### THE CONTRARIAN

The likeliest failure is feature breadth arriving before learning quality and account reliability. Accounts add a password and recovery problem; a larger curriculum adds a correctness and sequencing problem; more word banks add a content-maintenance problem. If all three are shipped at once, a learner may hit a broken sync and then see six shallow lessons presented as a full course. That damages trust more than a small course does. The worst realistic outcome is collecting email addresses and passwords, losing progress or locking learners out, and having no recovery path. The earliest warning is when account creation appears to work but a second browser cannot retrieve the exact same lesson and review state. Keep the browser copy as the source of immediate continuity, make server sync explicit, and ensure accounts can export and delete data. Keep the learning units short, reviewed, and honestly labeled as beginner material. Voice controls should select available Korean voices without claiming that every device has a human-quality narrator. Stop adding growth mechanics until question quality and sync are dependable.

### THE FIRST-PRINCIPLES THINKER

1. **The app must stay free — verified.** The creator explicitly made this a non-negotiable. Any design that needs a subscription to unlock core lessons is invalid.
2. **Learners need reasons to return — plausible.** Spaced review and saved progress are already in the product, but no retention evidence has been supplied.
3. **Accounts are the only useful way to sync across devices — untested.** Accounts enable sync, but may also create friction. Browser-local learning should remain available without them.
4. **More content alone will improve learning — untested.** Content needs ordered practice, feedback, and recall. A word list can help reference, but it does not prove mastery.
5. **Human-sounding speech can be generated for free — untested.** Device voices vary. The safe first step is to let learners choose available Korean voices and adjust pace; commissioned recordings or a hosted voice service need rights, funding, and a privacy decision.

Starting from zero, choose a small course that teaches a complete first interaction, remembers what the learner recalls, and keeps progress portable if an account is wanted. Defer paid speech infrastructure and social features until there is a real audience and a durable way to fund them without charging learners.

### THE EXPANSIONIST

The larger opportunity is a free, culturally thoughtful practice space rather than a generic vocabulary app. It can serve heritage learners who understand family speech but never learned Hangul, as well as complete beginners, without treating either group as the default. That opens a path toward optional placement, separate reading and speaking practice, and family or community-made lesson packs. The low-risk expansion is to make each lesson reusable: multiple-choice and typed recall, shuffled prompts, notes that explain why an answer fits, and a word bank that links words back to lessons and future reviews. Add account portability only as an option. Add export and deletion with it, so the app does not trap learning history. For pronunciation, select among Korean voices the device already has and make pace adjustable; use human recordings only after recruiting speakers and getting permission to publish them. This keeps the product useful while protecting the free promise. Option C is an offline-first course with optional sync accounts: new learners start immediately, while learners who need continuity can opt into an account. That preserves momentum without making personal data the admission price.

### THE OUTSIDER

This looks like a pleasant Korean app, but a new learner will ask three plain questions. “What do I do next?” The lesson path should show the next available step and why later steps are locked. “How do I know if I remember it?” Ask the learner to type an answer sometimes, then explain the correction; a familiar multiple-choice pattern is too easy to guess. “Will this work if I don’t make an account?” Yes: local practice must remain available, and the account screen should say what sync adds. A stranger will also wonder whether the speaking button records them. Make it explicit that it only plays device speech and never asks for a microphone. They may not recognize that a Korean-American learner can be both fluent at home and new to Hangul, or the reverse. Offer content in more than one modality and let the learner choose when to speak. Insiders may treat email/password login, shuffled distractors, and voice selection as implementation details. For the learner, the details matter only if the first lesson works, the answers make sense, and their progress does not disappear.

### THE EXECUTOR

Ship one coherent learning loop before expanding sideways: a learner can browse the path, take a lesson with mixed response types, get an explanation, meet words in context, review due cards, and see progress. Shuffle both question order and multiple-choice order once when a lesson opens; never shuffle on every render. Keep text answers forgiving about spaces and punctuation but specific enough to accept only intended answers. Add six authored lessons and a modest bank of useful words with romanization and example phrases. Add accounts using a database-backed session, a strong password hash, a throttling boundary, and progress scoped to the signed-in user. Keep local storage as a fallback and seed a new account from existing local progress. Provide export and account deletion. Improve speech without a new service: let a learner select an installed Korean voice and set a slower or faster pace. The cheapest useful next evidence is a five-person walkthrough of the first course, recording where people hesitate or misread answers; no paid service is needed. Before calling it ready for a broad public launch, exercise signup, sign-in, progress restore, logout, export, and delete against the real PostgreSQL migration, and add a recovery plan for forgotten passwords.

## THE CALL

Build the next free release around mixed retrieval practice, a short sequential beginner course, and optional account sync backed by PostgreSQL.

## CONFIDENCE

Medium. A walkthrough with intended learners that shows the path or account flow confusing them would change the order of work.

## WHY

The user explicitly requires a free beginner path. Typed recall, shuffled quiz order, and spaced review support active retrieval without a paid service. Optional accounts preserve no-login access while adding cross-device continuity.

## BIGGEST RISK

Passwords and progress create trust obligations. Use hashed passwords, expiring opaque sessions, server-side rate limits, account-scoped progress, export, deletion, and keep local progress available. A forgotten-password recovery flow remains a launch blocker for wider use.

## WHERE THE COUNCIL SPLIT

The Expansionist argues for a broad, culturally nuanced learning space; the Contrarian and Executor argue for a small, dependable loop. The call favors the smaller loop first because there is no learner research or curriculum validation yet.

## WHERE I MIGHT BE FOOLING MYSELF

The call matches the creator's wish to add more. Evidence for that scope is the existing free-first product brief, not a demonstrated demand for every feature. Keep optional accounts and local progress to reduce the risk of assuming everyone wants login.

## KILL CRITERIA

If learners cannot restore their progress after signing in on another browser, pause account rollout. If the device speech voices sound unusable to intended learners, present written pronunciation as the reliable path and source consented human audio before claiming natural speech.

More council follow-ups could stress-test this call, run a one-year pre-mortem, or rerun it with account creation required instead of optional.

## Features selected for this release

1. Six linked lessons across Hangul, syllable construction, greetings, grammar, and daily vocabulary.
2. Shuffled question order on each lesson start.
3. Shuffled multiple-choice answer positions.
4. Typed short-answer recall alongside multiple choice.
5. Normalized text matching for case, spacing, and punctuation.
6. A 25-word Korean bank.
7. Word-bank search across Hangul, romanization, and English meaning.
8. Category filters for greetings, people, food and drink, and everyday words.
9. Example phrases attached to vocabulary.
10. Lesson gating and completion tracking.
11. First-try recall stats in the dashboard.
12. Account registration, sign-in, sign-out, and optional cross-device progress sync.
13. Local-progress migration into a new account.
14. Password throttling and expiring HttpOnly sessions.
15. Account data export and deletion.
16. Device Korean-voice selection and saved speaking pace.
17. Review cards generated from completed lesson prompts and vocabulary.
18. Free, accountless browser-local practice remains available.

## Future feature ideas from the council

These are follow-on ideas, not promises or evidence of learner demand. Preserve the free learning path when evaluating each one.

1. Let learners choose a starting route such as “new to Hangul” or “I can speak a little,” without assuming skill from heritage or identity.
2. Add audio pairs that help learners notice similar Korean consonants and vowels.
3. Add optional Hangul handwriting practice with stroke order and self-checks.
4. Add a sentence builder for word order, particles, and polite endings.
5. Add short, branching dialogues for everyday situations.
6. Let learners save favorite phrases and add private notes.
7. Offer opt-in, quiet study reminders with flexible schedules.
8. Add downloadable offline lesson packs for travel and low-connectivity use.
9. Build review sessions around each learner’s missed or slow-to-recall material.
10. Show separate progress for reading, listening, vocabulary, grammar, and speaking practice.
11. Add culture and context notes reviewed by Korean-fluent contributors.
12. Commission short native-speaker recordings with explicit contributor permission and attribution.
13. Provide printable study sheets for Hangul, grammar patterns, and vocabulary.
14. Allow community-made lesson packs with moderation and clear sourcing.
